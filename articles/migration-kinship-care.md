# Leaving and Belonging: Seasonal Labour Migration and the Reconfiguration of Kinship and Care in Rural South Asia

**A conceptual and methodological article**

**Keywords:** circular migration; kinship; care; moral economy; reciprocity; multi-sited ethnography; South Asia

---

## Abstract

Seasonal and circular labour migration has become a defining feature of rural livelihoods in South Asia, yet anthropological accounts often treat migration as an economic strategy and kinship as a static backdrop against which that strategy unfolds. This article argues instead that circular migration and kinship are mutually constitutive: households make mobility possible through networks of obligation, and mobility in turn remakes who counts as kin, who is expected to care, and how reciprocity is measured. Drawing on classic work on the gift, moral economy, and relatedness, and on the more recent literature on migration and care, the article develops a three-part analytic framework — *obligation, absence, and repair* — for studying how migrant-sending households sustain intergenerational care across long periods of physical separation. It then translates the framework into a multi-sited ethnographic research design, including sampling logic, methods, ethical safeguards, and a set of testable propositions. The article does not report new fieldwork data; it is intended as a theoretically grounded basis for empirical studies and as a contribution to debates on how anthropologists should conceptualise mobility, dependency, and the household.

## 1. Introduction

Every year, large numbers of rural households in South and Southeast Asia send one or more members away for weeks or months to work in brick kilns, construction sites, agricultural belts, textile towns, and urban informal economies. Researchers of Indian labour markets have documented these flows for decades, noting that a great deal of migration is short-term, repeated, and poorly captured by censuses and surveys (Breman 1996; Deshingkar and Farrington 2009). Migrants seldom "leave" in the sense implied by classical models of rural–urban transition. They depart and return, and their departures and returns are organised around agricultural calendars, festival cycles, debts, and the life stages of the households they belong to.

Much of the policy and economics literature frames this movement in terms of remittances, wage differentials, and risk diversification. These framings are useful, but they leave a central question underexamined: *what happens to the social relationships that make a household a household when part of it is regularly absent?* Who cares for children and older people? How are obligations to distant kin negotiated when the person owed care is not physically present? And how do those who stay behind — often women, elders, and children — experience and interpret the absence of those who leave?

Anthropology has long provided tools for asking such questions. Studies of the gift showed that exchange is never merely the transfer of goods but the creation and maintenance of social bonds (Mauss [1925] 1990; Sahlins 1972). Work on the moral economy demonstrated that subsistence-oriented communities evaluate economic arrangements through shared norms of fairness and reciprocity (Scott 1976). Research on kinship has moved from the study of formal descent systems toward "relatedness," an approach that treats kinship as something made through everyday acts of feeding, sharing, and caring, not only given by blood or marriage (Carsten 2000). Meanwhile, studies of transnational and internal migration have shown that families stretch across space and that care can be organised at a distance, though often unequally and at emotional cost (Hochschild 2000; Parreñas 2005).

This article brings these strands together. Its central claim is that circular migration should be understood not simply as a household *strategy* but as a *practice that reconfigures kinship*. Households adapt their obligations, redefine caregiving roles, and renegotiate reciprocity in response to recurrent absence — and those adaptations in turn shape who is able to migrate, under what terms, and with what consequences for those left behind.

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literatures. Section 3 develops the analytic framework of obligation, absence, and repair. Section 4 sets out a multi-sited ethnographic research design. Section 5 offers a set of propositions the framework generates and discusses possible findings and their interpretation. Section 6 addresses ethics and reflexivity, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Migration as livelihood strategy

The "new economics of labour migration" and livelihoods approaches understand migration as a household decision taken to diversify income and manage risk. In the Indian context, scholars have shown that mobility is not an exceptional response to crisis but a routine element of many rural livelihoods — "multilocational" livelihoods spread across village, worksite, and sometimes several destinations (Deshingkar and Farrington 2009). Breman's (1996) study of "footloose labour" in western India emphasised that the mobile workforce is embedded in relations of dependency, debt, and intermediaries — contractors and labour brokers who recruit, advance money, and discipline workers.

This literature demonstrates the scale and structure of circular migration. However, it tends to treat the household as a unitary decision-maker or, at best, as an aggregation of individual economic agents. Feminist economists and anthropologists have long criticised this assumption, showing that households are sites of negotiation, hierarchy, and sometimes conflict, with unequal access to resources and voice. A framework that takes migration seriously as a social process must therefore open the household up and ask how relationships *within* it are organised and changed.

### 2.2 Reciprocity, gift, and moral economy

Mauss's ([1925] 1990) analysis of the gift established that exchange creates obligations to give, to receive, and to reciprocate, binding persons and groups together. Sahlins (1972) refined this by distinguishing generalised, balanced, and negative reciprocity along a continuum of social distance: among close kin, exchange tends to be generalised, with no strict accounting; among strangers, it approaches calculated or even predatory exchange. Bourdieu (1977) added that gift exchange depends on a temporal gap and on a collective "misrecognition" of the calculation involved: the gift must not appear to be immediately repaid, or it would cease to be a gift.

These insights are directly relevant to migration. Remittances can be understood as a form of generalised reciprocity among kin, but they are also subject to calculation: how much was sent, how often, and whether it matches what the migrant is thought to be earning. Scott's (1976) concept of the moral economy adds a collective dimension. Villagers hold shared expectations about what is fair — what a household may legitimately claim from relatives, patrons, or the community in bad times, and what those with more are morally expected to provide. When members leave for wage work elsewhere, they leave the immediate reach of these community-level sanctions and expectations, raising the question of how obligations are sustained, or eroded, in their absence.

### 2.3 Kinship as relatedness

Classical kinship studies focused on descent, alliance, and terminology. Later critiques argued that this approach reified "kinship" as a bounded domain and imposed Western assumptions about biology. Carsten's (2000) formulation of "relatedness" shifted attention to the ways people become kin through shared substance, co-residence, feeding, and everyday care. Strathern (1988) similarly challenged the Western opposition of individual and society by showing that, in Melanesian contexts, persons are understood as composite and relational.

For the study of migration, the relatedness approach has an important implication. If kinship is made through daily acts of care and commensality, then prolonged absence is not simply a *strain* on kinship; it is a condition under which kinship must be *actively re-made* through other means — phone calls, gifts, timed returns, festival attendance, and the redistribution of caring labour among those who remain.

### 2.4 Care, absence, and the "left behind"

Research on global care chains highlighted that the migration of carers creates care deficits and rearrangements elsewhere (Hochschild 2000). Parreñas (2005) documented the emotional experiences of children in Filipino transnational families, showing how mothering and fathering are reinterpreted across distance and how gendered expectations shape the burdens of migration. A growing body of research on internal and short-term migration extends these insights, though it is comparatively less developed for South Asian rural contexts, where extended family, caste and community networks, and state welfare structures interact in specific ways.

Two gaps stand out. First, much of the literature focuses on international migration and long-term separation; the rhythms of *seasonal* return — which create a distinctive pattern of intense presence followed by absence — remain less theorised. Second, most studies center either the migrant or the household left behind; fewer follow the relationship *between* the two across sites and over time.

### 2.5 Multi-sited method

Marcus (1995) argued that ethnography of mobile, dispersed phenomena requires "following" people, things, stories, or conflicts across multiple locations rather than confining study to a single bounded community. Gupta and Ferguson's (1997) call to rethink "the field" similarly questioned the assumption that culture is spatially anchored. Ferguson (2015), writing on distribution and social welfare in southern Africa, offers a further prompt: to attend to how people make claims on one another through "distributive labour" and to take dependency seriously as a social relation rather than a pathology. These methodological arguments motivate the research design set out in Section 4.

## 3. Analytic Framework: Obligation, Absence, and Repair

The framework has three interlinked components. Each is intended as a sensitising concept in the sense of directing empirical attention rather than prescribing outcomes.

### 3.1 Obligation

*Obligation* refers to the set of expectations — moral, customary, and sometimes contractual — that bind household members and wider kin to one another. Drawing on Sahlins (1972) and Scott (1976), obligations can be mapped along two axes: (a) *social distance* (from the co-resident nuclear unit to distant kin and community), which shapes whether reciprocity is expected to be generalised or balanced; and (b) *temporal horizon* (immediate exchange versus long-term, life-course reciprocity, such as the expectation that children will care for parents in old age).

Circular migration affects obligation in at least three ways: it *creates* new obligations (for example, debts to labour brokers or employers, which may compete with obligations to kin); it *intensifies* some (such as the expectation that a migrant will send money at particular times); and it *strains or defers* others (such as day-to-day eldercare or participation in community ritual).

### 3.2 Absence

*Absence* is not a mere void but a social condition with its own forms and meanings. Building on the relatedness literature (Carsten 2000), we treat absence as *structured* by several dimensions:

- **Duration and rhythm:** How long are people away, and how predictable is their return? Seasonal absence tied to an agricultural or industrial calendar differs from open-ended departure.
- **Communicative presence:** To what extent do mobile phones, voice and video calls, and messaging apps allow "co-presence" at a distance, and who controls access to these technologies within the household?
- **Material presence:** Money, food, clothing, and gifts sent or carried home as tokens of ongoing relationship.
- **Symbolic presence:** The ways an absent person is invoked in household decisions, ritual, or talk — for example, being consulted on marriage arrangements or included in ritual obligations even when not physically present.

Absence is also *differentiated by gender, age, and position*: the absence of a father, a mother, an adult son, or a daughter-in-law carries different meanings and generates different demands on those who remain.

### 3.3 Repair

*Repair* refers to the practices through which households and kin networks address the ruptures created by absence: redistributing caring labour, negotiating expectations, restoring relations after conflict, and marking return. Repair may be:

- **Material** (remittances, gifts, repayment of debts, hiring or exchanging labour to cover tasks left undone);
- **Relational** (reconciliation after conflict, renewed commitments, re-assignment of caring roles among siblings, in-laws, or neighbours);
- **Ritual and symbolic** (festival return, ceremonies of welcome and departure, participation in life-cycle events that "re-attach" the migrant to the kin group).

Repair is not always successful or equitable. Some households may repair effectively while others experience accumulating neglect, indebtedness, or estrangement. The concept thus directs attention to *variation* in outcomes and to the social resources — kin networks, community institutions, state programmes — that make repair possible.

### 3.4 Relations among the components

The three components form a cycle: obligations motivate departures and shape what is expected of the absent; absence generates gaps and tensions; repair practices seek to close those gaps and, in doing so, revise obligations. Over successive cycles, patterns of obligation may drift — for example, from generalised to more balanced and monetised forms of reciprocity — signalling deeper transformations in kinship. ## 4. Research Design

### 4.1 Research questions

1. How do rural households organise caregiving for children and older members during the seasonal absence of working-age adults?
2. How do migrants and those left behind negotiate, measure, and interpret reciprocity across distance?
3. Through what practices (material, relational, ritual) do households repair the disruptions of absence, and what determines the success of these practices?
4. How do these practices vary by gender, age, generation, caste or community position, and household composition?
5. Over repeated migration cycles, do patterns of obligation shift, and if so in what direction?

### 4.2 Multi-sited design

Following Marcus (1995), the study is designed around at least two linked sites: (a) one or more *sending villages*, and (b) one or more *destination worksites* (for example, a construction site or brick kiln cluster) to which villagers regularly travel. Access to destinations is best established by following particular families or work groups, rather than by seeking a general census of a worksite.

**Sampling.** A purposive, network-based sampling strategy is proposed. Beginning with a village-level mapping of households, the researcher identifies households with differing migration patterns: no migration, male-only migration, female or couple migration, migration with children, and migration of the young unmarried. Approximately 30–40 households are selected for in-depth engagement, with a subset of 8–12 followed to destination sites. The aim is analytic range, not statistical representativeness.

### 4.3 Methods

- **Extended participant observation** in the village across at least one full agricultural and migration cycle (ideally 12–14 months), with shorter, intensive periods at destination sites.
- **Household genealogies and care maps** documenting who lives where, who cares for whom, and how these arrangements change with departures and returns.
- **Repeated semi-structured interviews** with migrants, those left behind (with attention to women, elders, and children), and intermediaries such as labour contractors, timed to different points in the migration cycle.
- **Remittance and exchange diaries or recall protocols**, adapted to local literacy and trust: recording money, goods, and services exchanged, and — importantly — how participants describe and judge these exchanges.
- **Phone- and media-based ethnography**, with the informed consent of participants: observing how mobile communication is used to sustain relationships, who has access to phones, and what is said or withheld.
- **Observation of ritual and life-cycle events** (weddings, funerals, festivals) to see how absent members are included, excluded, or represented.

### 4.4 Analysis

Field notes and interview transcripts are coded iteratively, beginning with the sensitising concepts (obligation, absence, repair) and remaining open to emergent categories. Case-based comparison across households is used to identify configurations associated with more or less successful repair. Where the researcher works in local languages, transcription and translation decisions are documented, and key terms of kinship and obligation are retained in the original language with glosses to avoid flattening local meaning.

### 4.5 Illustrative operationalisation

| Concept | Dimension | Example indicators (qualitative) |
|---|---|---|
| Obligation | Social distance | Who is expected to give, receive, and host; differences in how close and distant kin are treated |
| Obligation | Temporal horizon | Expectations of old-age care; multi-year debts and loans among kin |
| Absence | Duration/rhythm | Months away per year; predictability of return dates |
| Absence | Communicative presence | Frequency, mode, and gatekeeping of calls and messages |
| Repair | Material | Timing, size, and form of remittances and gifts; reallocation of chores |
| Repair | Relational/ritual | Reconciliation practices; welcome/farewell rites; attendance at life-cycle events |

## 5. Propositions and Interpretive Possibilities

Because this article is conceptual, the following propositions are offered as hypotheses to be examined — and, where warranted, refuted — by empirical work. They are not findings.

**P1. Rhythmic absence produces "compressed reciprocity."** Where migrants return on predictable seasonal schedules, exchange and care obligations will tend to be concentrated in the return period (intense presence: feasts, repairs, decisions, settling of accounts), while ongoing day-to-day care is delegated to those who remain.

**P2. Care burdens are redistributed along existing gender and generational hierarchies.** Absence will not equalise caregiving; it will tend to intensify the workload of women and elders who remain, unless countervailing norms or institutions redistribute it. This proposition follows the broader findings on care and migration (Hochschild 2000; Parreñas 2005), but its specific patterning in South Asian rural households is an empirical question.

**P3. Debt to intermediaries competes with kin obligation.** Where migration is financed through advances from contractors or brokers (Breman 1996), migrants' capacity to meet kin obligations will be constrained and the moral economy of the village may come into tension with the market logic of the worksite.

**P4. Communication technologies expand symbolic presence but do not substitute for material and ritual presence.** Phone contact will help maintain a sense of relatedness, but households will continue to judge relationships by the presence of tangible support and participation in key events. Access to technology will itself be stratified.

**P5. Repeated cycles shift reciprocity from generalised toward balanced forms.** Over time, remittance flows may become more explicitly accounted for, and expectations more monetised, especially among more distant kin (following Sahlins 1972). Conversely, close kin — particularly parents and children — may retain generalised reciprocity, with contested boundaries between the two.

**P6. Effective repair depends on embeddedness in supportive networks.** Households with dense kin ties, reliable neighbours, and access to community institutions or state programmes will be better able to repair the ruptures of absence than isolated households (cf. Scott 1976; Ferguson 2015).

### 5.1 Interpreting divergent outcomes

The framework anticipates that the same structural pattern (seasonal migration) can yield very different kinship outcomes. A household might experience migration as an extension of family strength — a means of educating children, building a house, and meeting ritual obligations — or as a source of estrangement, indebtedness, and neglect of elders. Rather than expecting a single narrative of either "family breakdown" or "resilient adaptation," analysis should attend to the conditions under which one or the other emerges. This is especially important to avoid two common errors: romanticising the extended family as a seamless safety net, and pathologising migrant households as inherently fractured.

## 6. Ethics, Positionality, and Reflexivity

Research with migrant workers and their families raises particular ethical concerns. Migrants in precarious employment may fear reprisal from employers or contractors for speaking to researchers; those in debt bondage-like arrangements are especially vulnerable. Recommended safeguards include:

- **Informed consent** obtained orally or in writing in the participants' own language, with explicit discussion of confidentiality and the right to withdraw, and renewed at each site and phase.
- **Protection of identity:** anonymisation of individuals and, where necessary, of villages and worksites; careful handling of field notes, recordings, and phone data.
- **Avoiding harm at worksites:** negotiating access in ways that do not expose participants to employer scrutiny; being prepared to withdraw if presence puts participants at risk.
- **Attention to power within households:** interviewing women, elders, and children in settings where they can speak freely, and being careful not to reveal one household member's confidences to another.
- **Reciprocity and benefit:** considering how the research can return something to participants, whether through sharing findings in accessible form, connecting families with legal or welfare information, or supporting community-identified priorities.

Reflexivity is equally important. The researcher's own class, caste or community background, gender, language ability, and institutional affiliation will shape access and interpretation. Where researchers share a regional or cultural background with participants, insider status can enable trust but may also lead to unexamined assumptions; where they do not, they must work harder to understand local categories. In either case, positionality should be treated as part of the data and discussed openly in the resulting analysis (see Clifford and Marcus 1986 for the broader disciplinary debate on ethnographic authority).

## 7. Discussion and Conclusion

This article has argued for treating circular labour migration not merely as an economic strategy but as a *kinship-making and kinship-unmaking practice*. By foregrounding obligation, absence, and repair, the proposed framework allows researchers to trace how households sustain — and sometimes fail to sustain — relationships and care across long periods of separation. It bridges three literatures that often proceed in parallel: the anthropology of reciprocity and moral economy, the anthropology of kinship as relatedness, and the study of migration and care.

The framework has implications beyond anthropology. For **sociology and development studies**, it suggests that policies focusing narrowly on remittances or livelihood diversification risk missing the social costs and supports that make migration viable. For **political science and social policy**, it points to the role of state and community institutions — from seasonal hostels and crèches at worksites to portable welfare entitlements and pension schemes — in supporting repair. For **education**, it raises questions about how the schooling of children is affected by parental absence, and whether educational institutions can act as sites of care and continuity. For **geography**, it draws attention to the scalar and spatial organisation of households stretched across village and worksite.

The proposal has limitations. As a conceptual article it offers no empirical test of its propositions; the categories of obligation, absence, and repair may prove too broad or may need refinement in light of local idioms. The multi-sited design is demanding in time and resources, and access to worksites may be constrained. Finally, the framework was developed with rural South Asia in mind, and its transferability to other regions must be assessed rather than assumed.

Nevertheless, the central insight is a modest but consequential one: households do not simply *use* migration; they are *remade by it*, and they in turn remake the terms on which migration occurs. Understanding this reciprocal relationship requires ethnographic patience — attending not only to who leaves, but to who waits, who cares, who returns, and how everyone involved makes sense of the distance between them.
